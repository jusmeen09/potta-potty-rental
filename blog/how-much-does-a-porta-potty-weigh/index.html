How Much Does a Porta Potty Weigh?

An empty unit is a two-person lift. A full one is closer to a small car engine, and that difference is where planning goes wrong.

Star Portable Restrooms Rental Desk Published August 22, 2026 · Updated August 22, 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • A standard unit is roughly 150–200 lb empty on most models.
  • The tank dominates: waste weighs about 8.5 lb per gallon.
  • A full standard unit can approach 700 lb in total.
  • Weight matters for decks, rooftops, upper floors and any surface with a load limit.

  2. Why the tank dominates

Empty weight

A standard portable toilet is lighter than most people guess. Typical models land somewhere around 150 to 200 pounds empty, depending on manufacturer, wall thickness and whether the unit includes a urinal or extra fittings. That is a manageable two-person lift over a short distance, and it is why a delivery technician can reposition a fresh unit by hand.

Accessible units are substantially heavier, commonly in the region of 250 to 350 pounds empty, because the shell is considerably larger and includes internal grab rails and reinforcement. They are also bulkier, which affects how they are handled more than the raw figure suggests.

These are general ranges rather than specifications. Wall thickness, tank moulding and fittings all differ between manufacturers, and a unit built for heavy event hire is not the same shell as a budget model. If the exact number matters for your situation — a load calculation, a lift plan, a venue restriction — ask for the figure for the specific unit being delivered rather than working from an article.

Why the tank dominates

Empty weight is almost never the number that matters, because the tank is where the mass is.

A standard holding tank is typically around 60 gallons. Water weighs about 8.34 pounds per gallon and tank contents are slightly denser, so figure roughly 8.5 pounds per gallon in practice. A full 60-gallon tank is therefore in the region of 500 pounds on its own.

Add that to the shell and a fully loaded standard unit approaches 650 to 700 pounds. That is the figure to plan around wherever a load limit exists, and it explains why units are never moved when full — the weight is not the only issue, but sloshing several hundred pounds of liquid in a tall, narrow container is genuinely hazardous.

Restroom trailers are a different category

A restroom trailer is not a heavier portable toilet; it is a towed vehicle. Even a compact two-station trailer typically starts around 2,000 pounds dry, and larger multi-station units run to several thousand pounds before fresh water and waste are accounted for.

That changes the planning entirely. Towing capacity, a firm and level pad that will not sink under a point load, turning and manoeuvring clearance, and the surface bearing the weight all become real constraints. A trailer on a soft verge in wet weather is a recovery problem, not an inconvenience.

When the weight actually matters

For most placements — a job site, a field, a driveway, a car park — weight is not a consideration at all. There are four situations where it is decisive.

Decks, balconies and rooftops

Any elevated structure has a design load limit, usually expressed per square foot. A concentrated 700-pound point load on a residential deck is well outside what most are built for. Placement above ground level should be confirmed with the venue, the building owner, or a structural engineer — not assumed.

Upper floors of buildings

Same reasoning, plus the practical question of how a unit gets there and how a service hose reaches it. This is one of the main reasons indoor placement is usually the wrong answer.

Soft or unstable ground

Weight concentrated on a small footprint sinks into soft ground, and a unit that settles unevenly leans. After rain this can happen within a day. Ground protection matting spreads the load and is worth asking about for grass placements.

Surfaces you care about

Block paving, decorative concrete, resin driveways and finished floors can mark under a concentrated load, particularly in heat. Matting underneath solves it.

Moving a unit yourself

The honest advice is not to. An empty unit can be moved short distances by two people, but a unit in service is not empty, and the weight distribution of a partly full tank makes tipping easy and unpleasant.

If a unit needs relocating during a rental, call the operator. They will normally service it first — which removes most of the weight — and then reposition it properly. It is a routine request, usually quick, and considerably better than the alternative.

The related planning point: because relocating means servicing first, choosing the right position at the start is worth a few minutes of thought. That decision is also what determines whether the indoor placement question ever arises.
