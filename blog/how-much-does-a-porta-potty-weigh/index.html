How Much Does a Porta Potty Weigh?

Use the supplied model’s specifications and expected contents to assess weight. An empty figure is not an operating load.

Star Portable Restrooms Editorial Team Published August 22, 2026 · Updated October 9, 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways

  • PolyJohn lists the PJN3 with a plastic skid base at 165 lb empty.
  • The actual model and contents determine the operating load.
  • Obtain model-specific placement and handling information from the vendor.
  • Elevated or restricted surfaces need a site-specific assessment.

On this page

  1. Empty weight
  2. Why the tank dominates
  3. Restroom trailers are a different category
  4. When the weight actually matters
  5. Moving a unit yourself

Empty weight

Weight varies by model. As a documented example, PolyJohn lists the PJN3 with a plastic skid base at 165 pounds and a 60-gallon holding tank. Star does not guarantee that a vendor supplies this model.

Accessible units and other equipment have different weights and footprints. Ask for the exact model’s specification sheet, including accessories and liquids present at delivery.

A general article cannot approve a lift or a placement. Use the provider’s operating-load information for a site-specific assessment.

Why the tank dominates

For an illustration, 60 US gallons of water at about 8.34 pounds per gallon adds roughly 500 pounds. Added to a 165-pound shell, that is about 665 pounds.

This is a water-equivalent calculation, not a certified specification or a recommendation to fill the tank. Contents, safe fill limits, and accessories vary. Request the vendor’s expected operating load.

Restroom trailers are a different category

Restroom trailers have model-specific dry weights and rated limits. Ask for the model sheet and how water, waste, and accessories affect towing and placement.

That changes the planning entirely. Towing capacity, a firm and level pad that will not sink under a point load, turning and manoeuvring clearance, and the surface bearing the weight all become real constraints. A trailer on a soft verge in wet weather is a recovery problem, not an inconvenience.

When the weight actually matters

Check weight and stability whenever the surface, structure, or access route imposes limits.

Decks, balconies and rooftops

An elevated structure needs a site-specific assessment of load, footprint, access, and servicing. Obtain building-owner and qualified-professional approval; a general weight figure cannot establish suitability.

Upper floors of buildings

Same reasoning, plus the practical question of how a unit gets there and how a service hose reaches it. This is one of the main reasons indoor placement is usually the wrong answer.

Soft or unstable ground

Weight concentrated on a small footprint sinks into soft ground, and a unit that settles unevenly leans. After rain this can happen within a day. Ground protection matting spreads the load and is worth asking about for grass placements.

Surfaces you care about

Ask the owner and provider about appropriate surface protection. Matting does not automatically establish load capacity or prevent damage.

Moving a unit yourself

Ask the operator to relocate equipment according to the model instructions. Liquid contents change the load and balance.

If a unit needs relocating during a rental, call the operator. They will normally service it first — which removes most of the weight — and then reposition it properly. It is a routine request, usually quick, and considerably better than the alternative.

The related planning point: because relocating means servicing first, choosing the right position at the start is worth a few minutes of thought. That decision is also what determines whether the indoor placement question ever arises.
